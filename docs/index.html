PromptMoGe: metric geometry from one photo and a phone LiDAR, computed on the device

We prompt the MoGe‑3 geometry foundation model with the coarse LiDAR depth that every iPhone Pro and iPad Pro already produces, and compress the result until the whole pipeline — a ViT‑L, a sparse 3‑D refiner and a robust metric fit — runs on an iPad in about 150 ms.

Summary

Phones pair a sharp camera with a LiDAR that is metric but coarse (256×192), biased by a depth-dependent few percent, and blind to thin structures. Monocular geometry models have the opposite profile: MoGe‑3 recovers crisp relative shape, but its metric scale is a guess — on our ARKitScenes protocol its own scale head is off by 14.8 %, against 1.9 % for the raw sensor.

PromptMoGe joins the two. A four-channel LiDAR prompt enters a frozen DINOv2 backbone and MoGe‑3's decoder through zero-initialised gates, the network's scale-free depth is attached to the sensor by a closed-form robust fit that we differentiate through during training, and MoGe‑3's sparse 3‑D refiner is fed the confidence-gated disagreement with the sensor. Almost every component exists because a simpler version failed in a measured way, and we report those failures.

Two compressed models carry this to an iPad. Model A (480×640) is indistinguishable from the teacher on every benchmark we have; Model B (240×320) trades about 3 % accuracy for 23 ms. Both run with the Neural Engine, the GPU and custom int8 Metal kernels each doing the part they are fastest at, and agree with the PyTorch reference to 0.2 %.

Against PromptDA‑L, the strongest published LiDAR-prompted model, the honest summary is: better as released, a statistical tie in pooled error once both get the same sensor calibration, far better on thin and near structures, worse where the sensor is confidently wrong — with 1 200 tokens instead of 3 888, and on the device.

Approach

Starting point: MoGe‑3

MoGe‑3 [1] predicts an affine-invariant point map (x/z, y/z, log z): a DINOv2 ViT‑L/14 encoder, a normalisation-free convolutional neck that decodes 30×40 tokens up to 480×640 over five levels, and point, normal and mask heads. Its distinguishing part is self-guided sparse 3‑D refinement: every pixel is lifted to a voxel at (i, j, round(256·log z)) and a sparse 3‑D U‑Net, conditioned on the encoder feature, predicts an update to log‑depth, so features mix by 3‑D proximity instead of image proximity and depth edges stay sharp.

Aligned to the LiDAR by a robust scale and shift, stock MoGe‑3 reaches 3.5 % error on confident pixels — worse than the raw sensor (1.9 %). Relative shape alone is not enough; the sensor has to go into the network.

Zero-initialised gates, not zero-initialised projections

PromptDA [2] and ControlNet-style designs add the condition through a zero-initialised projection. Into a frozen ViT this failed twice: training diverged within 160 steps, and with a 5× lower rate it drifted instead — projection norms grew 35× in 160 steps while the gradient norm stayed small. The cause is Adam's step size: it moves every weight by roughly the learning rate per step whatever the gradient says, so a zero-initialised 256×1024 projection performs a growing random walk into the residual stream. We instead use a normally initialised projection multiplied by a per-channel gate initialised to zero, which bounds the injection with 1 024 numbers instead of 262 144 and gets its own, higher learning rate. A unit test asserts that the untrained model is bit-identical to MoGe‑3.

Where does the prompt matter? Trained from stock MoGe‑3 with everything else equal, removing the neck pyramid costs +17.8 % error on all 61 held-out rooms; removing the token injection is a tie (+0.5 %) except on thin structures (+2.2 %) (ablations).

Metric attachment that training cannot game

The network's depth lives in an arbitrary affine frame. We attach it with a trimmed least-squares scale and shift to the confident LiDAR pixels — four passes that keep the 80 % smallest relative residuals. Computing this fit with detached values and then applying a metric loss failed badly: the network shifts its global scale, the fit re-adapts, and training chases its own gauge (log‑depth inflated by 4.6 nats while the fitted scale collapsed from 0.83 to 0.01). Choosing the trimming weights from detached values but differentiating through the final solve makes the loss exactly invariant to the network's gauge and removed the problem.

Two optional inference-time refinements help with ARKit-grade sensors: a 12-knot lookup table that removes the LiDAR's depth-dependent bias against laser-scan ground truth (the sensor reads 3.0 % short at 0.35 m and 0.4 % short at 4.5 m — the signature of a time-of-flight range offset), and a per-frame quadratic gauge fitted robustly to the sensor. The calibration alone accounts for much of what any model gains over the raw sensor, so every comparison below states whether the baseline received it.

A refiner that sees the sensor

At 1 200 tokens the stock refiner barely changes depth. We give it two extra zero-initialised input channels: the log-residual between the re-aligned LiDAR and the current depth, and its gate κ = 0.15 + 0.85·conf. One step then lowers error in holes (−5 %), low-confidence regions (−4 %) and at edges (−3 %), and costs 4 % on thin structures — consistently, in 60 of 61 rooms. We therefore ship K = 1 and expose K = 0.

Training

What is trained. The DINOv2 backbone stays frozen in every model. The prompt stem, pyramid and gates, MoGe‑3's neck and heads, and (in a separate stage) the refiner are trained: 24 M of 374 M parameters. Seven attempts to adapt the backbone — LoRA, full fine-tuning with L2‑SP, feature distillation — gave no gain and more forgetting.

Data. Real frames come from ARKitScenes [3]: RGB, ARKit depth and confidence, and ground truth rendered from Faro laser scans (800 videos for most of the lineage, 1 570 videos / 157 k frames for the final stage). One batch slot in four is a Hypersim [4] frame with exact depth and normals and a learned sensor: a small network trained on 14 k real LiDAR/laser pairs that reproduces the real sensor's edge bias, correlated noise, hole sizes and confidence statistics. A frozen copy of stock MoGe‑3 supervises everything the sensor cannot.

Against copying the sensor. The easiest solution for a prompted model is to pass the prompt through. We counter it with prompt dropout (25 % of samples see an empty prompt and are distilled to stock MoGe‑3), simulated holes and low-confidence blobs, uncertainty-scaled depth noise, a 4× loss weight off-sensor, and a geometric-normal loss — without which the model reproduced sensor fuzz (depth normals 20.3° against 17.7° for stock MoGe‑3). Laser-scan edges sit a median 4.9 px from image edges, so ground truth within 3 px of its own depth jumps is masked, and an edge-gradient loss against the RGB-only teacher restores sharpness there; it is the source of most of the thin-structure gain.

Losses. Truncated log‑L1 after the differentiable fit; a low-pass fidelity term that keeps the output within 0.3 % of the confident sensor at coarse scale; scale-and-shift-invariant, multi-scale gradient, local-crop and boundary terms; teacher ties on rays, normals and mask. AdamW, cosine schedule, MoGe‑3's per-module bf16, batch 4 on one 24–48 GB GPU.

Running it on an iPad

Profiled module by module, the full model costs 296 ms per frame on an iPad Pro M5 — and its refiner's voxel structure alone took 82 ms to build. Two things close the gap to 152 ms: a smaller model that computes the same function, and a runtime that puts every piece on the processor that is fastest at it.

Compression without an accuracy bill (Model A)

  • Re-scheduled prompt pyramid. One 256→256 convolution at 480×640 was 84 % of the prompt path's 431 GFLOPs, feeding the neck level where the injection matters least (0.25 % of the activation it joins). Mirroring the neck's widths and regressing the new pyramid onto the old one's five outputs (two minutes of training) cuts the path to 29 GFLOPs with every metric within ±0.5 %.
  • Exact activation rescaling. The Neural Engine's fp16 accumulator overflows on the norm-free ReLU decoder. Because ReLU is positively homogeneous, dividing each block's inputs and biases by α and multiplying its output back is exact: peak activation 433 → 8, with a per-layer certificate that the accumulator bound is respected.
  • Refiner. The coarsest level cost 27 % of convolution time for 0.8 % of voxels and is halved in width; all 31 wide layers are trained quantisation-aware to the Metal kernel's exact int8 contract (per-channel weights, per-tensor activations, int32 accumulation), distilled from the uncompressed refiner's per-voxel updates — ground truth cannot protect edges that it masks. 39.4 M → 17.2 M parameters.

Model B drops one ×2 level from the neck, heads, pyramid and refiner — same 1 200 tokens, 240×320 output — warm-started by transferring the shared levels and refitting the head outputs by least squares.

Three processors, one frame

Self-attention at 1 200 tokens is where the Neural Engine loses: it retires softmax elements at a fixed rate, so the ViT takes 264 ms there. The GPU's fused attention never materialises the score matrix. We export every transformer block as two Core ML models — attention for the GPU, the MLP half for the engine — hand activations over through IOSurface-backed buffers without copies, and run the prompt pyramid on the engine while the GPU is busy with attention. The refiner has no Core ML form at all: voxelisation, pooling maps, 27-neighbour maps and per-tile tap masks are built on the GPU from a column-sorted layout in about 2 ms, instead of 60–80 ms on the CPU, and every residual block is three Metal dispatches around tiled int8 matrix multiplies.

Results

Unless noted, models run at 1 200 tokens with one refinement step, and errors are absolute relative error (AbsRel) against laser-scan ground truth with no alignment to it. PromptMoGe‑L is the full-size teacher; Model A and Model B are the on-device models. PromptDA‑L is our run of the public checkpoint at its native 756×1008 (3 888 tokens). “+ our attachment” gives PromptDA's output the same sensor calibration, robust fit and gauge our models use — the like-for-like comparison.

61 held-out rooms

Our main benchmark is 61 rooms (3 340 frames) of the ARKitScenes validation pool, split by physical room with the split fixed before any data was downloaded; none of the models below trained on them. Confidence intervals resample rooms.

Official ARKitScenes upsampling benchmark

On the 60 % of the official validation split that lies in our development partition (3 373 frames, Apple's protocol, ×4), our models match or beat our PromptDA‑L run on identical frames. We do not claim the published state of the art: our PromptDA run scores 0.0143 m where its paper reports 0.0132 m on the full split, a gap we could not explain by resolution or image format, while the sensor baseline reproduces to 1 %.

Zero-shot on seven other datasets

None of these datasets was used to train our models or PromptDA. The prompt is derived from ground truth: downsampled ×16, or ×8 with about 40 % of it removed in random rectangles (the more informative setting — with an exact dense ×8 prompt, plain interpolation beats every network). All models receive the same nearest-filled prompt and no intrinsics.

3‑D reconstruction

Depth maps are fused into a TSDF along the recorded ARKit trajectory and compared with the fusion of laser-scan depth, on 163 captures of the 61 rooms. Paired over rooms, PromptMoGe‑L beats PromptDA‑L as released on F@2cm (+0.009 [+0.004, +0.015]) and the calibrated sensor on every metric in 59–61 of 61 rooms; against PromptDA with our attachment it leads narrowly at the standard 4 cm voxel setting and ties at 2 cm; surface normals are 0.4–0.65° better than either PromptDA variant everywhere. Model A equals the teacher to within 0.0003 F‑score — compression is free for reconstruction. On three long captures, refining camera poses from each model's own depth (no ground truth) improves on ARKit's trajectory (ATE 1.55 → 1.34 cm) and gives F@2cm 0.856 for both our models against 0.846 for PromptDA with our attachment, whose surfaces are placed slightly more accurately (2.69 vs 2.83 cm).

Robustness to a degraded prompt

Perturbing the ARKit prompt identically for every model: our models are essentially immune to depth noise, because the robust fit averages it out and the network does not copy noisy values; misalignment (a timestamp or extrinsic error) is a tie with PromptDA + attachment; scattered dropout is handled by nearest-filling, which the released code and the app do by default.

Ablations

Sparse sensors and confidence

Two further results are not part of this release. A model trained only on ARKit's temporally integrated depth copies the smeared boundaries of a simulated single-pulse 112-beam sensor (confident error 1.2 % → 3.2 %, and its edges become worse than with the prompt ignored); redrawing a quarter of training prompts through a learned beam simulator recovers 13 % of that at +4 % on ARKit. And two small heads trained post hoc on frozen features predict the sensor's and the model's own per-pixel error without changing a single depth value; they rank errors well (they recover 50–70 % of the random-to-oracle sparsification gap) and one scalar per head calibrates the model-error head (its 90 % intervals then cover 91–92 %).

Limitations

  • Not better than PromptDA‑L everywhere. Under identical sensor attachment pooled accuracy is a tie. PromptDA is 17 % better where the sensor is confidently wrong and 4–9 % better at 2–4 m; our low-pass fidelity loss pulls the output toward every confident sensor pixel, including the wrong ones.
  • Indoor, short range. Training covers ARKit and Hypersim scenes within about 5 m. Outdoors (DIODE outdoor) PromptDA‑L is roughly twice as accurate.
  • RGB-only geometry erodes. With the prompt path bypassed, affine-invariant error on MoGe's own evaluation suite is 2–22 % worse than stock MoGe‑3 (worst on KITTI), because the decoder is fine-tuned. Metric scale and field of view are unaffected, and on the device a prompt is always present.
  • Ground-truth floor. On sensor-confident pixels the laser ground truth disagrees with its own multi-view fusion by 0.66 %, so differences below about 1 % there carry little weight. We could not reproduce PromptDA's published ARKitScenes number and label all PromptDA figures as our runs.
  • Refinement trades thin structures (+4 %) for everything else; the sparse-sensor results rest on a simulator, not hardware.

Related work

Prompted depth foundation models. PromptDA [2] fine-tunes Depth Anything V2, adding a min–max-normalised LiDAR map at four decoder scales through zero-initialised convolutions; it has no confidence input and trains on ARKitScenes and ScanNet++. Prior Depth Anything [5] pre-fills arbitrary priors before a conditioned refiner; LDCM [6] combines a Poisson pre-fill with a point-map head; SLIM [7] fuses sparse LiDAR into the neck of MoGe‑2 for driving. None reports latency on a phone or tablet; all time on datacenter GPUs. Depth completion networks (propagation, transformer and integration-based, e.g. OMNI‑DC [8]) are strong in domain but brittle to pattern shift; we instead condition a frozen foundation backbone. Conditioning. Our gates follow the zero-initialised lineage of ControlNet [9] and Flamingo's tanh gates [10], and like Flamingo keep the large pretrained model frozen. Alignment. The attachment combines MiDaS's closed-form alignment with its residual trimming [11], fitted to sensor points rather than ground truth and differentiated through.
